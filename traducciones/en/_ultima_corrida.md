### guion_largo

**ES:** El cerro del Tepeyac está en el extremo norte del antiguo valle de México, al pie de la sierra de Guadalupe. El cerro es parte de la sierra de Guadalupe. Hoy lo rodea la ciudad, pero en el siglo dieciséis era un lugar alejado de Tenochtitlan, al que se llegaba por una calzada. Esa calzada es la que hoy conocemos como Calzada de los Misterios. /  / Que los mexicas hayan construido un camino hasta este cerro dice algo: era un sitio al que se iba. En la tradición mesoamericana los cerros tenían con frecuencia un significado religioso, y el Tepeyac no era una excepción entre los cerros del valle. Hoy sabemos poco de lo que se hacía aquí, y lo poco que sabemos llega por otras vías. /  / La más conocida es la del fraile franciscano Bernardino de Sahagún. Hacia mil quinientos setenta y seis, en su obra sobre las cosas de la Nueva España, escribió que en el Tepeyac había un lugar donde se honraba a la diosa Tonantzin, y que hasta allí acudía gente de lugares lejanos. Tonantzin es un nombre náhuatl que significa nuestra madre. Sahagún cuenta que esa devoción lo inquietaba, porque el mismo nombre podía entenderse de dos maneras, y le preocupaba a quién estaban honrando quienes subían. /  / Esta es una información que viene de un solo autor, escrita varias décadas después de la conquista. Quienes viven la fe desde esa tradición no ven en el cerro un lugar que fue de otros, sino el lugar al que la Virgen vino a encontrarse con su pueblo. /  / Esta parada no resuelve la discusión de los historiadores. **EN:** Tepeyac hill lies at the northern edge of the ancient Valley of Mexico, at the foot of the Sierra de Guadalupe. The hill is part of the Sierra de Guadalupe. Today the city surrounds it, but in the sixteenth century it was a place far from Tenochtitlan, reached by a causeway. That causeway is the one we now know as the Calzada de los Misterios. /  / The fact that the Mexica built a road to this hill says something: it was a place people went to. In the Mesoamerican tradition, hills often had religious meaning, and Tepeyac was no exception among the hills of the valley. Today we know little about what was done here, and what little we know comes to us through other channels. /  / The best known is that of the Franciscan friar Bernardino de Sahagún. Around fifteen seventy-six, in his work on the things of New Spain, he wrote that at Tepeyac there was a place where the goddess Tonantzin was honored, and that people came there from distant places. Tonantzin is a Nahuatl name that means our mother. Sahagún tells us that this devotion troubled him, because the same name could be understood in two ways, and he worried about whom those who climbed the hill were honoring. /  / This information comes from a single author, written several decades after the conquest. For that reason historians read it with care, and each generation of scholars has returned to it to review what is known. Some think it shows continuity between the ancient place of worship and the Christian shrine. Others think the relationship between the two is harder to prove. Archaeological finds have been made on the hill, but their interpretation is also debated. /  / What can be said with certainty is this. Tepeyac was, from before the conquest, a site with religious meaning for those who lived in the valley. And it was, from the sixteenth century on, the place where the shrine of the Virgin of Guadalupe was built. /  / For the faithful, that shrine was born from an event. According to the Guadalupan tradition, in December of fifteen thirty-one the Virgin Mary appeared here to Juan Diego, an Indigenous man, and spoke to him in his own language. The Church recognizes the devotion to Our Lady of Guadalupe, and in two thousand two it canonized Saint Juan Diego in this very Basilica. Those who live their faith from that tradition do not see the hill as a place that once belonged to others, but as the place where the Virgin came to meet her people. /  / This stop does not settle the historians’ debate. It simply gives you the facts: an ancient road, a hill with worship, a friar’s concern, and a tradition that has gathered millions of people for almost five centuries.

## Pieza: bas_04_origen_culto_nican

### guion_corto

**ES:** La tradición guadalupana cuenta que en diciembre de mil quinientos treinta y uno la Virgen María se apareció a San Juan Diego en este cerro. Un texto de mil quinientos cincuenta y cuatro ya menciona una ermita en el Tepeyac. En mil seiscientos cuarenta y ocho se imprimió el primer relato extenso en español, y en mil seiscientos cuarenta y nueve el Nican mopohua, en náhuatl. Los historiadores discuten cuándo se escribió este último. Ambas cosas, la memoria y los documentos, forman esta historia. El santuario nace de la primera y se documenta en la segunda.

**EN:** Guadalupan tradition holds that in December of fifteen thirty-one the Virgin Mary appeared to Saint Juan Diego on this hill. That is the memory of faith, and it is the origin of the sanctuary. The surviving documents show another part of the story. A text from fifteen fifty-four already mentions a hermitage at Tepeyac. In sixteen forty-eight the first extensive account was printed in Spanish, and in sixteen forty-nine the Nican mopohua, in Nahuatl. Historians debate when the latter was written. Both things, the memory and the documents, make up this story. The sanctuary is born from the first and is documented in the second.

### guion_largo

**ES:** La historia del santuario se puede contar de dos maneras, y las dos son parte de lo que ves aquí. /  / La primera es la de la tradición guadalupana. Según ella, en diciembre de mil quinientos treinta y uno la Virgen María se apareció varias veces a un indígena llamado Juan Diego, en este cerro. Le pidió que fuera con el obispo, fray Juan de Zumárraga, y le dijera que quería un templo en este lugar. Como señal, la imagen de la Virgen quedó en la tilma de Juan Diego. According to the tradition recorded by the Basilica, the Vela recalls the vow of some sailors who were saved from danger at sea and who, to fulfill it, carried a mast up to this place and left it fixed in stone. It is a way of giving thanks similar to that of the votive offerings you saw in the museum. /  / The climb from the back of the Old Basilica to the chapel at the top is about one hundred meters. There are steps and sloping stretches, and it is best to take it slowly. If you take the garden path, you will walk among trees, water and bronzes. /  / According to the Mexico City tourism portal, this space is one of the quietest corners of the complex, except on feast days, when it fills with pilgrims. It is a place for those who want to go slowly, for those who seek a moment of silence and for those who simply want to rest a while between the noise of the esplanade and the summit. /  / As you climb, the city gradually opens up around you. Tepeyac is a small rise, but from it you can appreciate the expanse of the valley. /  / It is a space open to people of any belief, where you see families strolling and people sitting for a while. It is best to keep your voice low and your pace calm. /  / Some climb praying, and others climb to see the landscape. The garden has room for both ways of doing it. If you come across people in prayer, pass by without interrupting and without making noise.
