Morante, Garza, and Valencia measured it for years. Direct rays enter from late April until mid-August, around midday. The Sun passes through the zenith on May fourteenth. According to El Tlacuache, on that day the Sun is projected onto the floor for about forty-five minutes. The entry of light is distributed symmetrically around the summer solstice: fifty-two days before, the day of the solstice, and fifty-two days after. /  / Rubén Morante López, who has studied the site for about thirty years, explains that the movement of the Sun was followed in two places. One was the zenith, from this cave. The other was the horizon, with the Popocatépetl and the Cerro Jumil as references. Added together, those days make one hundred five, exactly what two hundred sixty lacks to reach three hundred sixty-five. For Morante, Garza, and Valencia, the two Mesoamerican calendars are marked in a single place. /  / For a time it was believed that this cave was just another quarry, like the eight others around the hill. But its vault and its hexagonal shaft, with an inclination calculated precisely, led people to think it was used to count time. According to Alvarado León, almost all studies agree on one thing. The cave served to observe the Sun’s passage through the zenith twice a year, and to measure the tropical year or mark the start of summer. What it was for is debated. Medina Romero proposes that the other two hundred sixty days are related to the ritual calendar. A note from the INAH says it helped correct the calendar. CyArk sees it as a way of following the Sun’s path toward the Tropic of Cancer. /  / The Xochicalca people did not enter where one enters today, but through a cave a few meters from the North Ball Court. Inside there was a chamber twenty-eight by ten meters, with three pillars carved into the rock. At the back is an oval chamber six meters across, the Chamber of the Astronomers, from which a second shaft rises. In nearby sectors, ceramic discs fifty-four centimeters wide, with holes, were found. Placed on the rim of the shaft, they let light through in small circles that move across the floor. /  / Today you cannot enter. The observatory is closed to the public, and reports from June two thousand twenty-six confirm it. Check at the entrance in case anything has changed. /  / But the idea can be imagined without going down. A city that, to measure time, did not only look at the sky. It let the sky into the mountain.
